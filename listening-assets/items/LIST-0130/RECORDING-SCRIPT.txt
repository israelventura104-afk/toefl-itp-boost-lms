TQB-LIST-0130 — Recording Script (Part C)
Topic: Volcanoes and Plate Boundaries
Voices: Narrator + Woman (Geology Professor)
Target length: ~60–90 seconds

Narrator: Listen to part of a talk in a geology class.

Woman (Professor): Volcanoes are not randomly scattered across Earth's surface.

Most of them form near the edges of tectonic plates—huge slabs of crust that move slowly over time.

At divergent boundaries, plates pull apart and magma rises to fill the gap, creating new crust.

The mid-ocean ridges are a classic example of that process under the sea.

At convergent boundaries, one plate can sink beneath another in a process called subduction.

Water carried down with the sinking plate helps melt rock above it, feeding chains of volcanoes like those around the Pacific “Ring of Fire.”

A smaller number of volcanoes form over hot spots, where a plume of heat rises from deep in the mantle.

As a plate drifts over a hot spot, a chain of islands can form—Hawaii is the usual classroom example.

So when you see a volcanic chain on a map, ask first: plate edge or hot spot?

Next time we'll talk about how magma chemistry changes eruption style—from gentle lava flows to explosive ash columns.

TQB-LIST-0132 — Recording Script (Part C)
Topic: The Invention of the Printing Press
Voices: Narrator + Woman (History Professor)
Target length: ~60–90 seconds

Narrator: Listen to part of a talk in a world history class.

Woman (Professor): Before the mid-1400s in Europe, books were copied by hand, mostly by monks and professional scribes.

That made books rare and expensive, so few people outside the elite could own one.

Around 1450, Johannes Gutenberg in Mainz combined movable metal type, oil-based ink, and a press adapted from wine presses.

His workshop produced the famous forty-two-line Bible, one of the first major books printed in the West.

The key idea was reusable type: once letters were cast, they could be rearranged for new pages instead of carving each page from scratch.

Within decades, printing shops spread across Europe, and the cost of books fell sharply.

Cheaper books helped literacy grow and sped the spread of scientific ideas, religious debates, and news.

Of course, printing did not invent writing—China and Korea had earlier printing traditions—but Gutenberg’s system transformed European publishing.

So when we talk about an information revolution in early modern Europe, this technology is usually at the center of the story.

Next lecture we’ll look at how printed pamphlets shaped politics in the sixteenth century.

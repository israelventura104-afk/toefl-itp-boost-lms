TQB-LIST-0128 — Recording Script (Part C)
Topic: Impressionism in 19th-Century Painting
Voices: Narrator + Woman (Art History Professor)
Target length: ~60–90 seconds

Narrator: Listen to part of a talk in an art history class.

Woman (Professor): In the 1870s, a group of French painters began working in a way that shocked many viewers.

Instead of finishing every detail in a studio, Impressionists often painted outdoors to catch changing light.

They used short, visible brushstrokes and brighter colors placed side by side.

From a distance, those strokes blend in the viewer’s eye; up close, the surface looks rough and unfinished.

Subjects were everyday scenes—cafés, train stations, rivers, and ordinary people—not only gods or kings.

Early critics mocked the style; the name “Impressionism” itself began as an insult after a painting titled Impression, Sunrise.

Dealers and independent exhibitions helped the artists reach buyers outside the official Salon.

Over time, the public grew used to the look, and museums later treated these works as modern classics.

What matters for us is the shift: painting became about momentary perception, not only polished storytelling.

Next week we'll compare Impressionism with Post-Impressionist approaches by Seurat and Cézanne.

Narrator: Now get ready to answer the questions.
